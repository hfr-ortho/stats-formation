## Median and interquartile range {#median-iqr}

**The question:** What's a typical Oxford Hip Score 1 year after hip replacement, and how much do scores vary?

### When to use it

- The variable is **skewed**, has a **ceiling or floor**, has **outliers**, or is **ordinal** (an ordered scale such as satisfaction from 1 to 5).
- The median is the middle value: half the patients are above it and half below. The **interquartile range (IQR)** runs from the 25th to the 75th percentile (the first and third quartiles), so it holds the middle half of the patients.
- If the variable is a roughly symmetric measurement → the [mean and SD](#mean-sd) are fine and more familiar.

::: {.callout-note collapse="true"}
## 🔍 Under the hood: where the median's CI comes from
The CI for a median uses no formula with an SD in it. It counts instead. Sort the scores, then pick two positions such that, if the study were repeated many times, the true median would fall between the scores at those positions at least 95% of the time. The binomial distribution says which positions those are, and the CI is the pair of scores sitting there. Because positions are whole numbers, the actual confidence is a little over 95% (R reports 0.9629 here). This "exact" method needs no assumption about the shape of the data.
:::

### How to report it

> **Methods:** Continuous variables that were skewed or bounded are reported as median (interquartile range, IQR).
>
> **Results:** The median Oxford Hip Score at 1 year was 42 points (IQR 39 to 46; 95% CI for the median 41 to 43).

::: {.callout-warning}
## ⚠️ Watch out: report the IQR as two numbers
Write "IQR 39 to 46", not "IQR 7" (the width). Readers want to know where the middle half of patients sits, not just how wide it is. And don't report a median with an SD: they belong to different summaries.
:::

::: {.callout-tip}
## 🔀 R vs Python: quartiles
R's `quantile()` and NumPy's `np.quantile()` use the same default method (R calls it "type 7"), so they agree. Other software doesn't always: gtsummary, SPSS and SAS can give slightly different quartiles on the same data ([page 2](../foundations/02-demographics.qmd#which-summary) shows an example). Say which software you used.
:::

## ⚠️ Watch out: percent of what?

::: {.callout-tip}

## Unpaired t test {#unpaired-t}

**The question:** Is the EFAS score 3 months after a midfoot injury different with a removable boot than with a cast?

### When to use it

- The outcome is a **measurement**, compared between **two independent groups**.
- Within **each group** it's roughly normal, or each group is large (about 30 or more) and not badly skewed ([page 3](../foundations/03-distributions.qmd#within-groups)).
- A score that adds up several questions, such as the EFAS score (six questions scored 0 to 4, so 0 to 24), can be treated as a measurement when few patients sit at its floor or ceiling.
- Use **Welch's** version, which doesn't assume the two groups have the same spread. It's R's default, but not Python's (see the R vs Python box).
- If the outcome is skewed, piles up at a floor or ceiling, or is ordinal → the [Mann-Whitney test](#mann-whitney).

## Mann-Whitney test {#mann-whitney}

**The question:** Is pain 6 weeks after a midfoot injury different with a removable boot than with a cast?

### When to use it

- The outcome is **skewed**, has a **ceiling or floor**, or is **ordinal**, and you're comparing **two independent groups**.
- It asks whether values in one group tend to be **larger** than in the other: if you picked one patient from each group at random, how likely is the first to score higher?
- It's the same test as the **Wilcoxon rank-sum test**. R calls it `wilcox.test()`.
- If the outcome is roughly normal within each group → the [unpaired t test](#unpaired-t).

### Look at the data first

The trial measured pain on a numeric rating scale (NRS) from 0 (no pain) to 10 (the worst pain imaginable), in whole numbers: an **ordinal** scale.

## 🔀 R vs Python: Mann-Whitney defaults

- **The Hodges-Lehmann CI.** R computes it by inverting the test; the Python code uses a bootstrap, a different method. With whole-number scores most resampled estimates are the same whole number (79% are exactly −1 here), and scipy's default bootstrap method (BCa) can't build an interval from them: it returns `nan` with a warning. The percentile method (`method="percentile"`) can. Here the two CIs agree (−2.00 to −1.00); on other data they can differ a little.

## Fisher's exact test and chi-square {#fisher-chi-square}

**The question:** Is graft failure within 2 years of an ACL reconstruction more common with a hamstring graft than with a bone-patellar tendon-bone (BPTB) graft?

### When to use it

- The outcome is **yes/no**, compared between **two independent groups**: a 2 × 2 table.
- **Fisher's exact test** is valid for any sample size. The **chi-square test** is a large-sample approximation: use it only when every *expected* count is at least 5. When in doubt, use Fisher's test.
- If patients were followed for different lengths of time and the event can happen at any time → the [log-rank test](#log-rank).

### Look at the data first

The ACL data hold one row per primary reconstruction. Everyone was followed for at least 2 years unless the graft failed first, so failure within 2 years (`failure_2y`) is known for every reconstruction. For two yes/no variables, "looking" means the 2 × 2 table, with the percentage in each group.

## 🔍 Under the hood: Yates' continuity correction
The chi-square test treats counts as if they could take any value, but counts are whole numbers. Both R's `chisq.test()` and scipy's `chi2_contingency()` apply it to 2 × 2 tables by default; without it (`correct = FALSE`, `correction=False`) p = 0.754. ## Paired t test {#paired-t}

**The question:** Do the frog-leg and Dunn 45° views give different alpha angles in the same hips?

### When to use it

- Each patient (or matched pair) gives **two measurements** of the same thing: here, the alpha angle of the same hip on two X-ray views.
- The **differences** between the two measurements are roughly normal, or there are about 30 or more pairs. The assumption is about the differences, not about each measurement on its own.
- Pairs are independent of each other.
- If the differences are skewed, or the measurements are bounded or ordinal → the [Wilcoxon signed-rank test](#wilcoxon-signed-rank).

### Look at the data first

The alpha angle measures how far the front of the femoral head-neck junction bulges (cam morphology): the larger the angle, the bigger the bump. The hip-preservation data hold one row per hip (one hip per patient), from young adults assessed for hip-preservation surgery, with the alpha angle measured on two views: the **frog-leg lateral** and the **Dunn 45°**.

Paired data need to be **wide**: one row per patient, with the two measurements side by side. These data already are; [page 1](../foundations/01-tidy-data.qmd#reshaping) shows how to reshape data that aren't.

Running an unpaired t test on the two columns ignores which readings belong to the same hip. Both are legitimate, but they answer different questions and aren't interchangeable: d~av~ compares the difference with how much hips differ from each other, d~z~ with how consistently the two views differ within a hip. We compute d~z~ directly so the two languages match; say which one you report.

## Chi-square test {#chi-square}

**The question:** Does return to sport 12 months after ACL reconstruction differ between bone-patellar tendon-bone (BPTB), hamstring and quadriceps tendon grafts?

### When to use it

- The outcome is **categorical** (here yes/no), compared across **three or more independent groups**: an r × c table.
- Every **expected** count should be at least 5. If not, combine categories that make sense together, or use Fisher's exact test, which R's `fisher.test()` runs exactly on larger tables too. scipy's `stats.fisher_exact()` accepts larger tables but estimates their p-value by random resampling, so it changes a little from run to run unless you give it a seed.
- With two groups and a yes/no outcome → [Fisher's exact test](06-two-unpaired-groups.qmd#fisher-chi-square).

### Look at the data first

- **p < 0.001:** differences this large would be very unlikely if all three grafts had the same rate of return to sport.

### Effect size and 95% CI

**Cramér's V** measures the strength of association in a table, from 0 (none) to 1 (perfect). For a table with 2 columns, about 0.1 is small, 0.3 medium and 0.5 large.
