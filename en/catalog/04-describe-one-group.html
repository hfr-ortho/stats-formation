4 · Describe one group

Mean and SD, median and IQR, a proportion, and a Kaplan-Meier survival curve, each with a 95% confidence interval.

Before you compare anything, describe it. This page covers the first row of the decision table: one number (or one curve) that sums up a group of patients, with a 95% confidence interval that says how precisely you know it. Which summary you use depends on the column, the type of data you have. Page 3 explains how to choose.

Note💡 How every section on this page works

Each section answers one question about one of the practice’s datasets: the joint replacement cohort, its patient-reported outcomes, or (for the proportion) the hip fracture data. Its first code block loads the packages and the data, so you can jump straight to the section you need. Run that section’s blocks in order, top to bottom.

To keep the code short, the joint replacement examples use every case, including both cases of patients who had both sides operated on (80 patients in the whole cohort; page 2 counts them). In a real study, decide how to handle them (page 3) and say what you did.

Mean and standard deviation

The question: How old are our patients, and how much do their ages vary?

When to use it

  • The variable is a measurement (age, BMI, operative time), not a category.
  • Its distribution is roughly symmetric, without extreme outliers. Check with a histogram (page 3).
  • If it’s skewed, has a ceiling or floor, or is ordinal → use the median and IQR instead.

What the calculation really does

The mean and the SD are two short pieces of arithmetic. The confidence interval adds a third.

  1. Mean: add up all the ages and divide by the number of cases.
  2. Distances: subtract the mean from each age. Some distances are positive and some negative, and together they always add up to 0, so they can’t simply be averaged.
  3. Variance: square each distance, which makes them all positive. Add the squares up and divide by n − 1, one less than the number of cases.
  4. Standard deviation (SD): take the square root of the variance. That brings the result back to years: the typical distance of a patient’s age from the mean.
  5. Confidence interval (CI): divide the SD by √n to get the standard error of the mean, then go about 2 standard errors either side of the mean.

Once the numbers are in, a box under “Read the output” does each step by hand.

Read the output

  • n = 600: the number of cases with an age recorded.
  • Mean = 65.5 years: the average age.
  • SD = 9.4 years: the standard deviation, the typical distance of a patient’s age from the mean. In a bell-shaped distribution about 95% of patients lie within 2 SDs of the mean, here 65.5 ± 2 × 9.4, roughly 47 to 84 years.
  • 95% CI 64.8 to 66.3: the confidence interval for the mean. It tells you how precisely 600 patients pin down the average age of patients like ours. It says nothing about how spread out individual patients are; that’s the SD’s job.

The first case was 56 years old, which is 56 − 65.52 = −9.52 years from the mean. The variance comes to 89.02, and its square root is the SD the software printed, 9.44 years. The standard error is 0.385 years and the exact multiplier is 1.964, so the CI is 65.52 ± 1.964 × 0.385: from 64.76 to 66.28, as in the output.

The multiplier is called t. It is close to 1.96 for large samples and a little bigger for small ones. Quadruple the sample size and the standard error halves; the SD stays about the same.

How to report it

Methods: Continuous variables are reported as mean (standard deviation, SD) when approximately normally distributed.

Results: The mean age at surgery was 65.5 years (SD 9.4; 95% CI for the mean 64.8 to 66.3).

Warning⚠️ Watch out: SD or SEM?

The standard error of the mean (SEM) is the SD divided by √n: 9.44 / √600 ≈ 0.39 years here, much smaller than the SD of 9.4 years. That is why some papers report “mean ± SEM”: it looks tidier. But it describes the precision of the mean, not your patients. Use the SD to describe patients and a 95% CI to show the precision of an estimate. Never report a bare “±” without saying which one it is.

Tip🔀 R vs Python: dividing by n or n − 1

R’s sd() and pandas’ .std() divide by n − 1 (the “sample” SD), which is what you want. NumPy’s np.std() divides by n unless you write np.std(x, ddof=1). With 600 patients the difference is tiny; with 10 it isn’t.

Median and interquartile range

The question: What’s a typical Oxford Hip Score 1 year after hip replacement, and how much do scores vary?

When to use it

  • The variable is skewed, has a ceiling or floor, has outliers, or is ordinal (an ordered scale such as satisfaction from 1 to 5).
  • The median is the middle value: half the patients are above it and half below. The interquartile range (IQR) runs from the 25th to the 75th percentile (the first and third quartiles), so it holds the middle half of the patients.
  • If the variable is a roughly symmetric measurement → the mean and SD are fine and more familiar.

What the calculation really does

The median and the quartiles come from sorting, not from adding.

  1. Sort the scores from lowest to highest.
  2. Median: take the score in the middle of the sorted list. With an even number of scores there are two middle scores, and the median is halfway between them.
  3. Quartiles: do the same a quarter and three quarters of the way along the list. The interquartile range runs from the first of these scores to the second.
  4. Only the order matters, so extreme scores don’t move the median: the lowest score could drop to 0 and the middle of the list would stay where it is.

Once the numbers are in, a box under “Read the output” finds the same scores by their position in the list.

Both middle scores are 42, so the median is 42. The quartiles sit at positions 49.25 and 145.75. The 49th and 50th scores are both 39, and the 145th and 146th are both 46, so the quartiles are 39 and 46.

The CI for a median uses no formula with an SD in it. It counts instead. Each patient is as likely to score above the true median as below it, like a coin toss. The binomial distribution, which describes coin tosses, says that out of 194 patients, fewer than 83 landing on one side would happen less than 2.5% of the time. So the 83rd score from the bottom is rarely above the true median, and the 83rd from the top (the 112th from the bottom) is rarely below it. Those two scores are 41 and 43, the CI in the output. Because positions are whole numbers, the confidence comes out a little over 95%: 0.9629, the number R reports. This “exact” method needs no assumption about the shape of the data.

How to report it

Methods: Continuous variables that were skewed or bounded are reported as median (interquartile range, IQR).

Results: The median Oxford Hip Score at 1 year was 42 points (IQR 39 to 46; 95% CI for the median 41 to 43).

Warning⚠️ Watch out: report the IQR as two numbers

Write “IQR 39 to 46”, not “IQR 7” (the width). Readers want to know where the middle half of patients sits, not just how wide it is. And don’t report a median with an SD: they belong to different summaries.

Tip🔀 R vs Python: quartiles

R’s quantile() and NumPy’s np.quantile() use the same default method (R calls it “type 7”), so they agree. Other software doesn’t always: gtsummary, SPSS and SAS can give slightly different quartiles on the same data (page 2 shows an example). Say which software you used.

Proportion

The question: What proportion of hip fracture patients died within 30 days?

When to use it

  • The outcome is yes/no (binomial): death within 30 days, complication, discharge home.
  • Report the count, the percentage, and a 95% CI for the percentage.
  • If patients were followed for different lengths of time and the event can happen at any time (revision, death) → use a Kaplan-Meier curve instead. Here every patient was followed for at least 30 days or until death, so the 30-day proportion is complete (“Look at the data first” checks this).

What the calculation really does

A proportion is one count divided by another. The work is in its confidence interval.

  1. Count the patients with the event, and the patients whose outcome is known.
  2. Divide the first count by the second. That is the proportion; multiply it by 100 for a percentage.
  3. The simple recipe for the CI is the proportion ± 1.96 standard errors, with the standard error worked out from the sample’s own proportion. This is the Wald interval, and it works badly when the event is rare or the group is small.
  4. The Wilson interval turns the question around. It collects every true proportion from which our observed proportion would lie no more than 1.96 standard errors away, and it works the standard error out from each of those candidates in turn. The result is not centered on the observed proportion: it is pulled a little toward 50%, and it can never go below 0% or above 100%.

Once the numbers are in, a box under “Read the output” calculates both intervals by hand.

The Wald interval would run from 0.048 to 0.075. The Wilson interval runs from 0.049 to 0.077, as in the output. It sits a little higher because its center is 0.0631, not our proportion of 0.0617. The last line shows what defines it: the lower limit, 0.0494, lies 0.0123 below our proportion, and 1.96 standard errors worked out from 0.0494 are also 0.0123. With 74 deaths among 1,200 patients the two intervals are close. With fewer patients or a rarer event they move apart.

How to report it

Methods: Categorical variables are reported as number (percentage). 95% confidence intervals for proportions were calculated with the Wilson score method.

Results: Within 30 days of admission, 74 of 1,200 patients died (6.2%; 95% CI 4.9% to 7.7%).

Warning⚠️ Watch out: percent of what?
  • Say what the denominator is. Here it’s 1,200 patients: the hip fracture data have one row per patient. In the joint replacement cohort, rows are procedures, and the 80 patients who had both sides operated on (page 2 counts them) count twice, so a percentage there is a percentage of procedures. If some outcomes were missing, the denominator is the patients with a known outcome; say how many were missing.
  • Don’t use the simple “± 1.96 standard errors” CI (the Wald interval) for proportions. For rare events it can dip below 0%. The Wilson interval doesn’t.
Tip🔀 R vs Python: which CI for a proportion?

R’s prop.test() gives the Wilson interval, but adds a continuity correction unless you set correct = FALSE. scipy’s proportion_ci() calls the uncorrected version "wilson" and the corrected one "wilsoncc". R’s binom.test() and scipy’s method="exact" give the more conservative Clopper-Pearson interval (page 5 uses it). All are acceptable; say which you used.

Kaplan-Meier survival curve

The question: What proportion of implants are still unrevised 5 years after surgery?

When to use it

  • The outcome is time until an event (revision, death), and patients were followed for different lengths of time.
  • Some patients are censored: their follow-up ended (the study closed, they moved away, they died) before any revision. We know they went at least that long without one.
  • A plain percentage (81 of our 600 cases were revised, 13.5%; the next step counts them) ignores follow-up time: a patient operated on last year hasn’t had time to need a revision. The Kaplan-Meier method uses each patient’s follow-up for as long as it lasts.

Page 13 covers survival analysis in depth. This section shows the basic curve.

What the calculation really does

The Kaplan-Meier method builds the curve one revision at a time.

  1. Put the cases in order of follow-up time. At any moment, the cases still being followed and not yet revised are at risk.
  2. Each time a revision happens, work out the fraction of the cases at risk that got past that moment without one: 1 − revisions ÷ cases at risk.
  3. Multiply these fractions together, from the day of surgery up to the time you are interested in. The product is the estimated proportion still unrevised at that time. Plotted against time, it is the curve.
  4. A censored case counts among those at risk until its follow-up ends, and then leaves the count. It never makes the curve step down. But every later revision is divided by fewer cases at risk, so each later step is a little bigger.

Once the curve is drawn, a box under “Read the output” rebuilds the 5-year estimate by hand.

Read the output

  • The curve starts at 100% and steps down at each revision. Censored patients don’t make it step; they just leave the group “at risk”.
  • The shaded band is the 95% CI. It widens to the right as fewer patients remain under follow-up.
  • The numbers at risk under the plot show how many patients were still being followed at each time. Trust the right-hand end of the curve less: by 10 years only 21 of the 600 cases remain (the last number in the first row under the plot).
  • At 5 years: survival 0.863 (95% CI 0.828 to 0.892), with 263 patients still at risk (n.risk in R, the last number Python prints).
  • The rest of what is printed: in R’s line, n.event 66 is the number of revisions up to 5 years (81 over the whole follow-up) and std.err is the standard error of the estimate. How to report it

Methods: Implant survivorship was estimated with the Kaplan-Meier method, with revision for any reason as the end point. Patients were censored at death or at their last follow-up.

Results: Revision-free survival at 5 years was 86.3% (95% CI 82.8% to 89.2%; 263 patients at risk).

Warning⚠️ Watch out: death isn’t just “lost to follow-up”

Treating deaths as censored, as here, assumes the patients who died would have gone on to be revised at the same rate as everyone else. They can’t be: a patient who has died can never be revised. When many patients die during follow-up, 1 minus the Kaplan-Meier estimate overstates the risk of revision. Page 13 shows the competing-risks method that fixes this.

Tip🔀 R vs Python: the confidence band

R’s survfit() uses a “log” CI by default; lifelines uses “log-log”. The estimates are identical but the CIs differ slightly. We set conf.type = "log-log" in R so the two match. Log-log keeps the CI between 0% and 100%; say which you used.

The tables under the two plots differ by one case at 4 years and at 8 years (332 and 84 in R, 331 and 83 in Python). R counts a case whose follow-up ends exactly at that time as still at risk; lifelines doesn’t. For the same reason lifelines’ own count at 5 years would be 258, not 263: the follow-up of 5 cases ends at exactly 5 years. The Python code above counts the way R does. lifelines also adds a row for the cases censored so far.
