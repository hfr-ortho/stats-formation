6 · Compare two unpaired groups

Unpaired (Welch’s) t test, Mann-Whitney test, Fisher’s exact and chi-square tests, and the log-rank test, each with an effect size and 95% CI.

Two groups of different patients: a removable boot vs a cast in a trial, hamstring vs BPTB grafts, implant A vs implant C. This is the most common comparison in clinical research. If the same patients are measured twice, or patients were matched in pairs, use page 7 instead.

Note💡 How every section on this page works

Each section answers one question about one of the practice’s datasets: a randomized trial of a cast vs a removable boot after a midfoot injury (the t and Mann-Whitney tests), ACL reconstructions (Fisher’s test) and the joint replacement cohort (the log-rank test). The trial and its results are invented for teaching. Each section’s first code block loads the packages and the data, so you can jump straight to the section you need. Run that section’s blocks in order, top to bottom.

To keep the code short, the joint replacement example uses every case, including the 80 patients who had both sides operated on. In a real study, decide how to handle them (page 3) and say what you did.

Unpaired t test

The question: Is the EFAS score 3 months after a midfoot injury different with a removable boot than with a cast?

When to use it

  • The outcome is a measurement, compared between two independent groups.
  • Within each group it’s roughly normal, or each group is large (about 30 or more) and not badly skewed (page 3).
  • A score that adds up several questions, such as the EFAS score (six questions scored 0 to 4, so 0 to 24), can be treated as a measurement when few patients sit at its floor or ceiling.
  • Use Welch’s version, which doesn’t assume the two groups have the same spread. It’s R’s default, but not Python’s (see the R vs Python box).
  • If the outcome is skewed, piles up at a floor or ceiling, or is ordinal → the Mann-Whitney test.

Look at the data first

Effect size and 95% CI

  • The mean difference with its CI is the effect size readers understand best: 3.6 points (95% CI 2.4 to 4.8).
  • Hedges’ g expresses the difference in standard deviations, which lets you compare effects measured on different scales. As a rough guide, 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large.

Hedges’ g is 1.13: the boot arm’s mean is more than one standard deviation higher, a large effect. Whether 3.6 points on a 24-point score matters to patients is a clinical question, not a statistical one.

How to report it

Methods: EFAS scores at 3 months were compared between the arms with Welch’s unpaired t test, in the patients seen at that visit.

Results: At 3 months, the mean EFAS score was 17.4 points (SD 2.9; n = 57) with a boot and 13.8 (SD 3.4; n = 55) with a cast (difference 3.6 points, 95% CI 2.4 to 4.8; Hedges’ g = 1.13, 95% CI 0.73 to 1.52; p < 0.001).

Warning⚠️ Watch out: don’t test the variances first

An old habit is to run a test of equal variances (such as Levene’s) and then choose Student’s or Welch’s t test depending on the result. Don’t: the two-step procedure distorts the p-value. Use Welch’s test from the start. When the variances really are equal, it gives almost the same answer as Student’s.

Tip🔀 R vs Python: Student’s or Welch’s?
  • The default differs. R’s t.test() runs Welch’s test unless you add var.equal = TRUE. scipy’s ttest_ind() runs Student’s test unless you add equal_var=False. Always set it explicitly in Python.
  • The order of the groups. R subtracts in the order of the factor levels (we put boot first); scipy subtracts the second argument from the first.
  • The CI for Hedges’ g. effectsize uses an exact method and pingouin an approximation, so the CIs can differ slightly (0.73 to 1.52 in R, 0.73 to 1.53 in Python). The estimates agree to five decimals: both correct g for small samples, effectsize with the exact formula and pingouin with the usual approximation.

Mann-Whitney test

The question: Is pain 6 weeks after a midfoot injury different with a removable boot than with a cast?

When to use it

  • The outcome is skewed, has a ceiling or floor, or is ordinal, and you’re comparing two independent groups.
  • It asks whether values in one group tend to be larger than in the other: if you picked one patient from each group at random, how likely is the first to score higher?
  • It’s the same test as the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. R calls it wilcox.test().
  • If the outcome is roughly normal within each group → the unpaired t test.

Look at the data first

The trial measured pain on a numeric rating scale (NRS) from 0 (no pain) to 10 (the worst pain imaginable), in whole numbers: an ordinal scale.

Read the output

  • W = 1074.5 (R) or U_val = 1074.5 (Python): the Mann-Whitney U statistic for the boot arm, the number of (boot, cast) pairs in which the boot patient reported more pain, counting ties as half. There are 58 × 58 = 3,364 pairs, so a U of 1,682 would mean no tendency either way. R calls it W.

  • difference in location −1.00, 95% CI −2.00 to −1.00 (R only): the Hodges-Lehmann estimate, below. R finds it by a numerical search, so it prints −1.000041 and the like; the last digits are noise.
  • RBC = −0.361 (Python): the rank-biserial effect size, below.

Effect size and 95% CI

  • The Hodges-Lehmann estimate is the median of the differences between every boot patient’s score and every cast patient’s score: a typical difference, in points.
  • The rank-biserial correlation (r) runs from −1 (every boot patient reported less pain than every cast patient) to +1 (the reverse). 0 means no tendency either way.

# Hodges-Lehmann estimate: the median of every boot-minus-cast difference
def hodges_lehmann(x, y):
    return np.median(np.subtract.outer(np.asarray(x), np.asarray(y)))

print(hodges_lehmann(pain_boot, pain_cast))
-1.0
# scipy has no CI for it, so use a bootstrap: recompute it on 9999 resampled datasets.
# method="percentile": with whole-number scores, scipy's default method can't build this CI (see the R vs Python box)
hl_boot = stats.bootstrap((pain_boot.to_numpy(), pain_cast.to_numpy()), hodges_lehmann, vectorized=False,
                          method="percentile", rng=np.random.default_rng(2026))
print(hl_boot.confidence_interval)
ConfidenceInterval(low=np.float64(-2.0), high=np.float64(-1.0))

With a boot, pain was typically 1 point lower (95% CI 2 points to 1 point lower), and r = −0.36. Of all 3,364 (boot, cast) pairs, the boot patient reported less pain in 61%, more in 25%, and 14% were tied. With whole-number scores every difference between two patients is a whole number, so the Hodges-Lehmann estimate and its CI land on whole numbers too, and the estimate can sit on the edge of its own CI, as here.

How to report it

Methods: Pain at 6 weeks (NRS 0 to 10, an ordinal scale) was compared between the arms with the Mann-Whitney test, in the patients seen at that visit, with the Hodges-Lehmann estimate of the difference and its 95% CI as the effect size.

Results: At 6 weeks, pain was a median of 4 points (IQR 3 to 4.75; n = 58) with a boot and 5 (IQR 4 to 6; n = 58) with a cast (Hodges-Lehmann difference −1 point, 95% CI −2 to −1; rank-biserial r = −0.36, 95% CI −0.53 to −0.17; p < 0.001).

Warning⚠️ Watch out: it isn’t a test of medians

The Mann-Whitney test asks whether one group’s values tend to be larger, not whether the medians differ. Two groups can have the same median and a significant Mann-Whitney test (if their shapes differ), or different medians and a non-significant one. Report the medians to describe the groups, but don’t write “the medians differed significantly”.

Tip🔀 R vs Python: Mann-Whitney defaults
  • Exact or approximate. With ties (as here) both programs use a normal approximation. Without ties, R computes an exact p-value for groups under 50, scipy only for very small groups. We set exact = FALSE in R and method="asymptotic" in Python so they always match.
  • Continuity correction. Both programs apply it by default for the approximation; we set it explicitly anyway.
  • The Hodges-Lehmann CI. R computes it by inverting the test; the Python code uses a bootstrap, a different method. With whole-number scores the estimate barely moves: leaving out any one patient still gives −1. scipy’s default bootstrap method (BCa) needs those leave-one-out estimates to vary, so here it returns nan with a warning. The percentile method (method="percentile") doesn’t need them. Here the two CIs agree (−2.00 to −1.00); on other data they can differ a little.

Fisher’s exact test and chi-square

The question: Is graft failure within 2 years of an ACL reconstruction more common with a hamstring graft than with a bone-patellar tendon-bone (BPTB) graft?

When to use it

  • The outcome is yes/no, compared between two independent groups: a 2 × 2 table.
  • Fisher’s exact test is valid for any sample size. The chi-square test is a large-sample approximation: use it only when every expected count is at least 5. When in doubt, use Fisher’s test.
  • If patients were followed for different lengths of time and the event can happen at any time → the log-rank test.

Look at the data first

The ACL data hold one row per primary reconstruction. Everyone was followed for at least 2 years unless the graft failed first, so failure within 2 years (failure_2y) is known for every reconstruction. For two yes/no variables, “looking” means the 2 × 2 table, with the percentage in each group.

Note🔍 Under the hood: Yates’ continuity correction

The chi-square test treats counts as if they could take any value, but counts are whole numbers. For a 2 × 2 table, Yates’ correction subtracts 0.5 from each |observed − expected| difference before squaring, which makes the p-value larger (more cautious). It’s meant to bring the p-value closer to Fisher’s, but it can overshoot, as here: each observed count is only about half a count from its expected count (6 failures against 6.5 and 5.5 expected), so the corrected statistic is almost 0 and p = 0.986, against Fisher’s 0.775. Both R’s chisq.test() and scipy’s chi2_contingency() apply it to 2 × 2 tables by default; without it (correct = FALSE, correction=False) p = 0.754. Either is acceptable if you say which you used; with small counts, prefer Fisher’s test.

  • The risk difference: the difference between the two percentages, in percentage points. It’s what patients and clinicians understand.

How to report it

Methods: Graft failure within 2 years was compared between hamstring and BPTB grafts with Fisher’s exact test. Effect sizes are reported as risk differences with Wald 95% CIs and as odds ratios with exact 95% CIs.

Results: Graft failure within 2 years followed 6 of 154 hamstring reconstructions (3.9%) and 6 of 129 BPTB reconstructions (4.7%) (risk difference −0.8 percentage points, 95% CI −5.5 to 4.0; odds ratio 0.83, 95% CI 0.22 to 3.20; p = 0.775).

Warning⚠️ Watch out: “no difference” needs a narrow CI

The odds ratio’s CI runs from 0.22 to 3.20: the data are compatible with a hamstring graft having a fifth of the odds of failure of a BPTB graft, or three times the odds. With only 12 failures, this study can’t tell. “No significant difference” is the honest wording; “hamstring and BPTB grafts fail equally often” isn’t.

Tip🔀 R vs Python: two different odds ratios

scipy’s fisher_exact() reports the simple sample odds ratio (a × d) / (b × c), 0.8311 here. R’s fisher.test() reports the conditional maximum-likelihood odds ratio, 0.8316, which goes with its exact CI. Use scipy.stats.contingency.odds_ratio(..., kind="conditional") to get R’s number and CI. The two are always close; say which you report.

Log-rank test

The question: Is revision-free survival different between implant A and implant C?

When to use it

  • The outcome is time until an event (revision), patients were followed for different lengths of time, and you’re comparing two independent groups.
  • It compares the whole survival curves, not the survival at one time point.
  • The Mantel-Haenszel test (or Mantel-Cox test) in the decision table is the same test under another name.
  • It’s most powerful when one group’s risk is a steady multiple of the other’s over time (proportional hazards). If the curves cross, see page 14.
  • To compare three or more groups, or to adjust for other variables → Cox regression.

How to report it

Methods: Revision-free survival was estimated with the Kaplan-Meier method and compared between implants with the log-rank test. Hazard ratios were estimated with Cox proportional hazards regression.

Results: Revision-free survival at 5 years was 71.1% (95% CI 61.5% to 78.8%) with implant C and 89.9% (95% CI 84.7% to 93.3%) with implant A (log-rank p < 0.001; hazard ratio 3.04, 95% CI 1.83 to 5.04).

Warning⚠️ Watch out: overlapping CIs aren’t a test

Comparing the two curves’ CIs at one time point (“the 5-year CIs overlap, so there’s no difference”) isn’t a valid test, and neither is comparing the crude percentages revised. Use the log-rank test on the whole curves. And as on page 4, deaths here are treated as censored; page 13 covers competing risks.

Tip🔀 R vs Python: one formula or two groups

R’s survdiff() takes a formula with a grouping variable. lifelines’ logrank_test() takes each group’s follow-up times and events separately (multivariate_logrank_test() takes a grouping column). They compute the same test. Both Cox models handle tied revision times with Efron’s method, so the hazard ratios agree.
