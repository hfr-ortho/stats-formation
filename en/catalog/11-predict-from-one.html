11 · Predict a value from another variable

Simple linear and nonlinear regression, nonparametric regression, simple logistic regression and Cox regression: how an outcome changes with one predictor.

A correlation (page 10) says how strongly two things go together. Regression says how much the outcome changes for each unit of the predictor, and lets you predict the outcome for a new patient. Which regression depends on the outcome: a measurement on a straight line or a curve, a measurement that isn’t normal, a yes/no outcome, or time until an event. With several predictors at once, see page 12.

Note💡 How every section on this page works

Each section answers one question about one of the practice’s datasets: the joint replacement cohort and PROMs, hip fracture for logistic regression, ACL reconstructions for Cox regression. Its first code block loads the packages and the data, so you can jump straight to the section you need. Run that section’s blocks in order, top to bottom.

To keep the code short, the joint replacement examples use every case, including both cases of patients who had both sides operated on (80 patients in the whole cohort; page 2 counts them). In a real study, decide how to handle them (page 3) and say what you did.

Simple linear regression

The question: How much longer does the operation take for each extra point of BMI?

When to use it

  • The outcome is a measurement, and you want to know how much it changes per unit of one predictor, or to predict it.
  • The relationship is roughly a straight line, and each patient’s outcome is independent of the others’.
  • The residuals (each patient’s distance from the line) are roughly normal, with a similar spread all along the line. Check them after fitting.
  • If the relationship curves → nonlinear regression. If the outcome is skewed or has a ceiling → nonparametric regression. If it’s yes/no → logistic regression.

What the test really does

Linear regression draws the straight line that fits the points best, and then asks how sure we can be of its slope.

  1. For any line you could draw, measure each patient’s vertical distance from it: the residual.
  2. Square the residuals and add them up. The least-squares line is the one line for which this sum is as small as possible. Software finds it with a formula, not by trial and error.
  3. The slope of that line says how much the outcome changes, on average, for each extra unit of the predictor. The line always passes through the point where both variables are at their mean.
  4. The test asks whether the slope is further from 0 than chance would explain: t = slope ÷ its standard error, turned into a p-value. The standard error is smaller with more patients, with less scatter around the line, and with a wider spread of the predictor.

Once the model has run, a box under “Read the output” rebuilds the line by hand.

Effect size and 95% CI

The slope with its 95% CI is the effect size: 1.37 minutes per kg/m² (95% CI 1.15 to 1.58). Scale it to a difference that matters clinically: 5 kg/m² of BMI goes with 5 × 1.368 = 6.8 more minutes (95% CI 5 × 1.151 = 5.8 to 5 × 1.585 = 7.9). The slope and both ends of its CI are multiplied by 5, using the unrounded numbers in the output. R² = 0.20 says how much of the variation the line explains.

How to report it

Methods: Operative time was modeled on BMI with simple linear regression.

Results: The slope of operative time on BMI was 1.37 minutes per kg/m² (95% CI 1.15 to 1.58; p < 0.001; R² = 0.20), or 6.8 minutes (95% CI 5.8 to 7.9) per 5 kg/m².

Warning⚠️ Watch out: the line is an average, and only inside your data

The line predicts the average operative time at each BMI. Individual patients scatter around it by about 15 minutes either way (the residual standard error), so it’s a poor tool for scheduling one patient’s case. And BMI here runs from 18.0 to 44.5 (printed under “Look at the data first”): don’t use the line to predict outside that range.

Nonlinear regression

The question: How quickly does KOOS JR recover after knee replacement, and where does it level off?

When to use it

  • The outcome follows a curve whose shape you know in advance, and the curve’s numbers mean something: here a starting score, a plateau and a recovery rate.
  • You supply the formula and rough starting guesses, and the program finds the values that fit best (nonlinear least squares).
  • If you don’t know the shape → splines or LOWESS. If it’s a straight line → linear regression.

The recovery curve used here:

\[\text{score} = \text{plateau} - (\text{plateau} - \text{start}) \times e^{-\text{days}/\tau}\]

  • start: the score on the day of surgery.
  • plateau: the level the score settles at.
  • τ (tau): the time constant. After τ days patients have made 63% of their eventual gain (1 − e−1 = 0.63), and half of it after τ × ln 2 ≈ 0.69 τ days.

What the test really does

Nonlinear regression fits a curve the way linear regression fits a line: by making the squared distances between the points and the curve as small as possible.

  1. Start from your rough guesses for the curve’s numbers (here plateau, start and τ) and draw that curve.
  2. Measure each score’s vertical distance from the curve, square the distances and add them up.
  3. Change the numbers a little in the direction that makes the sum smaller, and repeat. For a curve there is no formula that gives the answer in one step, which is why the program needs starting guesses.
  4. Stop when no small change makes the sum any smaller: the fit has converged. The numbers at that point are the estimates.

Once the model has run, a box under “Read the output” shows the sum for the starting guesses and for the fitted curve.

Warning⚠️ Watch out: each knee appears up to four times

Each knee has up to four scores (pre-op, 6 weeks, 3 months and 1 year). Nonlinear least squares treats the 1146 scores as 1146 independent patients, but they come from 330 knees, and scores from the same knee are related. So these CIs can’t be trusted: depending on the number, they may be too narrow or too wide. A mixed model (page 16) accounts for this. And if the fit fails to converge, try starting guesses closer to what the plot shows.

Tip🔀 R vs Python: two kinds of CI
  • R’s nls() and scipy’s curve_fit() use different fitting algorithms but find the same estimates and standard errors.
  • R’s confint() profiles the fit: it moves each number away from its estimate until the fit gets significantly worse. scipy gives only the standard errors, so the Python code uses estimate ± t × standard error. The two CIs differ slightly; here τ’s CI runs from 71.0 to 92.5 days in R and 69.9 to 91.8 in Python, so the CI for the half-gain time is 49 to 64 days in R and 48 to 64 in Python. Either is fine; say which you used.

Nonparametric regression

The question: How does KOOS JR at 1 year depend on KOOS JR before surgery, when many patients reach the ceiling of 100?

When to use it

  • The outcome is a measurement that isn’t normal: it has a ceiling, a skew or outliers, so the residuals of a straight line won’t be normal.
  • LOWESS (locally weighted smoothing) draws the shape without a formula: it fits many small lines, each to the points near it, and joins them into a smooth curve. It gives a picture, not a p-value.
  • The Theil-Sen slope is the median of the slopes between every pair of patients. A few extreme patients barely move it, and its CI comes from ranks, not from normal residuals.
  • If the residuals are roughly normal → linear regression.

What the test really does

This section uses two tools, and neither assumes normal residuals.

  1. LOWESS takes one patient at a time and fits a straight line to the patients whose pre-op score is nearest (here the nearest two-thirds), giving the closest ones the most weight. It keeps only that line’s height at the patient’s own pre-op score.
  2. Joining these heights gives a smooth curve that follows the data wherever they go. LOWESS gives a picture, with no slope and no p-value.
  3. The Theil-Sen slope takes every pair of patients and works out the slope of the line through the two of them. The 246 knees here make 30135 pairs.
  4. It then takes the median of all these slopes. A few unusual patients change only a few of the pairwise slopes, so they barely move the median.

The R code under “Run it” does steps 3 and 4 by hand.

# Sen's 95% CI: step in from both ends of the sorted slopes by an amount set by the
# variance of Kendall's S, corrected for tied scores (the same method as scipy)
tie_term <- function(v) { counts <- table(v); sum(counts * (counts - 1) * (2 * counts + 5)) }

m <- length(slopes)
ts_ci <- c(slopes[max(round((m - step) / 2), 1)], slopes[min(round((m + step) / 2) + 1, m)])

Tip🔀 R vs Python: Theil-Sen in R, and LOWESS settings
  • Theil-Sen in R: some R packages get the CI wrong when scores are tied, as PROM scores always are (deming 1.4-1 misapplies its tie correction; mblm’s CI is far too narrow). So the R code computes the slope and Sen’s CI directly, the same way scipy does, and the two agree exactly.
  • Intercept: scipy’s default (method="separate") computes it as median(y) − slope × median(x). The R code computes median(y − slope × x), which scipy calls method="joint". The slope is the same either way.
  • LOWESS: R’s lowess() and statsmodels’ lowess() run the same algorithm, but statsmodels’ defaults differ (it sets delta to 0), so the Python code spells out R’s settings. ggplot2’s geom_smooth() uses a different smoother, loess(), which draws a similar but not identical curve.

Simple logistic regression

The question: Does the risk of death within 30 days rise with the time to surgery?

When to use it

  • The outcome is yes/no (died or not), and you want the odds ratio per unit of one predictor.
  • It assumes the log odds change in a straight line with the predictor: each extra day of delay multiplies the odds by the same amount.
  • You need enough patients with the outcome: a common rule of thumb is at least 10 per predictor.
  • If the outcome is time until an event → Cox regression.

What the test really does

Logistic regression draws a straight line too, but on a scale where a yes/no outcome can follow one: the log odds.

  1. A risk can’t follow a straight line for long, because it has to stay between 0% and 100%. So the model works with the odds (the risk divided by 1 minus the risk) and then with their logarithm, the log odds, which can take any value.
  2. The model assumes the log odds change in a straight line with the predictor: log odds = intercept + coefficient × predictor.
  3. It tries out values for the intercept and the coefficient, and keeps the pair under which the outcomes that really happened (who died and who survived), taken together, were most likely. This is called maximum likelihood.
  4. Adding the coefficient to the log odds is the same as multiplying the odds by e raised to the coefficient. That number is the odds ratio per unit of the predictor.

Once the model has run, a box under “Read the output” turns the two coefficients into predicted risks by hand.

Cox regression

The question: Does the hazard of ACL graft failure rise with posterior tibial slope?

When to use it

  • The outcome is time until an event (graft failure), and the predictor is a measurement (tibial slope).
  • Cox regression gives a hazard ratio per unit of the predictor. Choose a unit that means something: here, per degree.
  • It assumes the hazard ratio stays the same over time (proportional hazards) and that the log hazard changes in a straight line with slope. Page 14 shows how to check both and covers Cox regression in full.
  • Deaths are censored in any Cox model of another event. That hardly matters in young ACL patients, who rarely die during follow-up. In older patients it does: the hazard ratio then describes the rate among patients still alive, and a patient’s actual chance of the event needs the cumulative incidence or a Fine-Gray model.

What the test really does

The Cox model works as on page 8, with a measurement in place of groups.

  1. Start at surgery and move forward in time. Each time a graft fails, look at who was still being followed just before it: the patients at risk.
  2. The model assumes each extra degree of slope multiplies the hazard by the same factor, the hazard ratio. A patient whose slope is 2° steeper has the hazard multiplied by that factor twice.
  3. It tries out values for the hazard ratio and keeps the one under which the failures that really happened, taken together, were most likely: at each failure, was it one of the steeper slopes among the patients at risk?
  4. The test asks whether that hazard ratio is further from 1 than chance would explain, and gives a p-value.

A patient who leaves the study early is censored: they count among the patients at risk for as long as they were followed, and not after.

2. A colleague asks whether older hip fracture patients are more likely to die within 30 days. Which regression would you use, and what do you find?

TipSolution

The outcome is yes/no and the predictor is a measurement: simple logistic regression. 74 deaths are plenty for one predictor.

  • Python

Results: In 270 hips (906 scores), the fitted curve gave an estimated HOOS JR of 45.6 points (95% CI 44.2 to 46.9) at surgery and a plateau of 87.9 points (95% CI 86.3 to 89.4); half of the change was reached at about 37 days (95% CI 33 to 41).

The CIs are R’s profile CIs; Python’s, computed from the standard errors, round to the same numbers here.

Hips seem to recover faster than knees (half the gain by about 5 weeks, against 8). To test that difference, fit both in one model (page 12).
