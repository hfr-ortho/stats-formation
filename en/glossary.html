Glossary

Plain-language definitions of the statistical terms used on this site.

Terms with a dotted underline on any page link here. Hover over one to read its definition without leaving the page.

Every definition comes from a published reference. The list of sources names the reference behind each entry.

Aalen-Johansen estimator
A method for estimating the probability of being in each of several states (for example unrevised, revised, or dead without revision) at each time. With competing risks it gives the cumulative incidence. With a single type of event it gives the same result as the Kaplan-Meier method.
Adjusted (for other variables)
Estimated while accounting for the effect of other variables, usually by putting them into a regression model together with the variable of interest. An implant’s hazard ratio adjusted for age and sex is the effect of the implant that is left once the effects of age and sex have been taken out. An unadjusted estimate leaves the other variables out.
Agreement
How close repeated measurements of the same patient are to each other, for example by how many degrees two raters’ readings of the same knee differ. It is not the same as correlation: two raters’ readings can be perfectly correlated while one always reads higher. For continuous measurements it is shown with Bland-Altman limits of agreement.
ANOVA (analysis of variance)
A test that compares the means of three or more groups at once. It compares the variation between the group means with the variation among patients within the groups; their ratio is the F statistic. One-way ANOVA has a single grouping variable, such as implant type, and a small p-value says that at least one group mean differs, not which one.
Association
Two variables are associated when they show some relationship with each other, for example when patients with a higher BMI tend to have longer operations. In a positive association the two tend to rise together; in a negative one, one tends to fall as the other rises. An association does not by itself show that one causes the other.
Assumption
A condition that a test or model takes for granted about the data, for example that patients are independent of each other, that the values follow a normal distribution or that groups have equal variances. The p-value and confidence interval can be trusted only as far as the assumptions hold, so they need to be checked.
At risk
Still being followed, and not yet having had the event, at a given moment.
Baseline
The starting point of a study: when the patient enters it, before the treatment being studied. Baseline characteristics are what was recorded then, such as age, sex and the score before surgery. A later measurement minus the baseline measurement is the change from baseline.
Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment
A way of adjusting p-values that controls the false discovery rate instead of the family-wise error rate. That is a less strict standard, so it usually misses fewer real differences than Holm’s or Bonferroni’s adjustment. Often shortened to BH.
Bias
A systematic error: something in the design, conduct or analysis of a study that pushes an estimate away from the true value in one direction. Unlike the play of chance, it would still be there on average if the study were repeated many times. In a Bland-Altman analysis, the bias is the mean difference between the two methods or raters.
Binary (yes/no) outcome
An outcome with only two possible values, such as revised or not revised. It is summarized as a proportion: the share of patients in whom the answer is yes.
Binomial test
An exact test that compares one group’s proportion of a yes/no outcome with a hypothetical proportion. It uses the binomial distribution, which gives the probability of each possible number of patients with the outcome (0, 1, 2 and so on) out of a fixed number of patients, when each patient has the same chance of it.
Bland-Altman limits of agreement
A way of showing how far apart two measurements of the same thing can be, such as readings by two raters or two methods. The differences between the paired measurements are plotted against their means: the mean difference is the bias, and about 95% of the differences fall within the limits of agreement (the bias plus and minus 1.96 standard deviations of the differences). Whether the limits are narrow enough is a clinical judgment, not a statistical one.
Bonferroni correction
The simplest adjustment for multiple comparisons: every p-value is multiplied by the number of tests, which is the same as dividing the 0.05 threshold by that number. It controls the family-wise error rate, but it is strict, so real differences are missed more easily. Holm’s adjustment gives the same protection and is never stricter.
Bootstrap
A way to get a confidence interval without a formula. The computer draws many new samples of the same size from the data themselves, so that some patients appear more than once and others not at all, and recalculates the estimate on each. The spread of those estimates gives the interval; the percentile method and BCa are two ways of reading it off.
Bounded scale
A scale whose values cannot go below a lowest or above a highest possible value, such as a score from 0 to 100. Values on such a scale often pile up at one end or at both, so the distribution is often skewed and rank-based (nonparametric) methods are commonly used.
Boxplot
A plot that sums up a variable’s values, usually with one box per group. The box runs from the first to the third quartile, so it holds the middle half of the values, and a line inside it marks the median. Lines called whiskers reach to the lowest and highest values within 1.5 times the IQR of the box; values beyond them are drawn as separate points.
Categorical variable
A variable whose values are categories, not measured numbers, such as sex, graft type or ASA class. The possible categories are called its levels. When the levels have a natural order, the variable is ordinal.
Ceiling (or floor) effect
Many patients score the best (or worst) value a scale allows, so the scale cannot show how much better (or worse) they really are.
Censored
Followed for a while without having the event, then no longer observed: the study ended, the patient moved away, or the patient died without the event, when death is not the event studied (see the entry Competing risk). All we know is that the event had not happened by then. The usual methods assume that censored patients have the same chance of a later event as those still being followed.
Central limit theorem
A mathematical result: when samples are large enough, the means of repeated samples follow a normal distribution even if the individual values do not. This is why tests on means, such as the t test and ANOVA, still work for values that are not normally distributed, provided the sample is large enough.
Change score
A patient’s score after treatment minus the same patient’s score before it (at baseline), so each patient contributes one number: how much they changed. Say which score is subtracted from which, because that sets the sign.
Chi-square (χ²) statistic
A test statistic that adds up how far the observed counts are from the expected counts: each difference is squared and divided by the expected count. The larger it is, the further the data are from the null hypothesis. The chi-square distribution, whose shape depends on the degrees of freedom, turns it into a p-value, and some other tests, such as Kruskal-Wallis, use that distribution too.
Chi-square test
A test for counts in categories that compares the counts observed with the expected counts. For a contingency table, these are the counts expected if the two variables had nothing to do with each other; in the goodness-of-fit version, they are the counts that hypothetical proportions predict for one group. Its p-value is an approximation that needs reasonably large expected counts.
Clopper-Pearson interval
A confidence interval for a proportion, also called the exact interval, calculated from the exact probabilities of each possible count (the binomial distribution) instead of from an approximation. It is guaranteed to capture the true proportion at least as often as stated, which makes it conservative: usually a little wider than it needs to be.
Clustered data
Data in which observations come in groups (clusters) whose members are more alike than members of different groups: patients of one hospital, repeated scores of one patient, or the two knees of one patient. An analysis that ignores the clustering makes the results look more precise than they are. Clusters inside larger clusters, such as knees within patients, are called nested.
Cochran’s Q test
A test for a yes/no outcome measured three or more times in the same patients, or once in each member of a matched set. It asks whether the proportion answering yes is the same at every visit, and extends McNemar’s test to more than two measurements.
Coefficient
The number a regression model estimates for a predictor: how much the outcome changes, on average, when that predictor goes up by one unit and the other predictors stay the same. For a categorical predictor it is the difference from the reference group. In logistic and Cox regression the coefficient is on a logarithm scale, and software converts it into an odds ratio or a hazard ratio.
Cohen’s d
An effect size for the difference between two means: the difference divided by the standard deviation of the two groups, pooled into one. It counts the difference in standard deviations instead of points, so effects measured on different scales can be compared. For paired data, Cohen’s dz divides the mean change by the standard deviation of the changes.
Cohen’s kappa (κ)
A measure of how well two raters agree when they sort the same patients into categories, after allowing for the agreement expected by chance alone. It is 1 for perfect agreement and 0 for agreement no better than chance. Weighted kappa is the version for ordered categories: a near miss counts as a smaller disagreement than a big miss.
Cohort
A defined group of patients who are followed over time, such as everyone who had a hip replacement at one hospital during given years. In a cohort study, what happens to them is recorded, and subgroups, such as patients with different implants, are often compared.
Common language effect size (CLES)
The probability that a randomly chosen patient from one group has a higher value than a randomly chosen patient from the other group, with a tie counted as half. It is also called the probability of superiority. A value of 0.5 means that higher and lower are equally likely.
Competing risk
An event that makes the event of interest impossible. A patient who dies can never have a revision, so death is a competing risk for revision. Counting such patients as censored makes 1 minus the Kaplan-Meier estimate overstate how many patients will have the event.
Complete-case analysis
An analysis that uses only the patients who have a value for every variable it needs and leaves out everyone else. It loses precision because fewer patients are analyzed, and it can be biased when the patients left out differ from those kept.
Concordance (C-index)
How often a model ranks two patients in the right order: of all the pairs that can be compared, the share in which the patient the model rated at higher risk was the one who had the event sooner. A value of 0.5 is what random guessing would give, and 1 is a perfect ranking. It is also called the C-index or c statistic.
Confidence interval (CI)
A range of values for the true effect that fit the data. A 95% CI comes from a method that captures the true value in 95 of 100 repeated studies.
Confounding
A distortion of a comparison between groups, caused by a third factor (the confounder) that is related both to the group a patient is in and to the outcome. If older patients more often receive one implant and also have more complications, age confounds the comparison of the implants. Adjusting for the confounder in the analysis reduces the distortion.
Conover’s test
A post-hoc test used after the Friedman test to compare the visits or conditions in pairs. It works on the same kind of ranks as the Friedman test, and its p-values are then adjusted for multiple comparisons.
Conservative
Erring on the side of caution. A conservative test or correction gives p-values that are, if anything, too large. A conservative confidence interval is, if anything, wider than necessary: it captures the true value at least as often as it claims.
Contingency coefficient
A number that says how strongly two categorical variables in a contingency table are linked, calculated from the chi-square statistic: 0 means no link, and larger values mean a stronger link. Phi and Cramér’s V are the common ones. Pearson’s contingency coefficient (C) is another, whose largest possible value is below 1 and depends on the size of the table.
Contingency table
A table of counts that sorts patients by two categorical variables: one variable defines the rows, the other the columns, and each cell holds the number of patients with that combination. Also called a cross-tabulation. A 2 × 2 table has two rows and two columns, for example graft type by failure (yes or no).
Continuity correction (Yates)
A small adjustment that software makes when it uses a chi-square or normal approximation for counts or ranks: it moves the observed value half a unit closer to the expected value before calculating the test statistic. This usually makes the p-value a little larger. Yates’ correction is the version for a 2 × 2 table.
Continuous variable
A variable measured on a numeric scale that can take any value within a range, such as age, BMI or operative time. Scores with many possible values are usually analyzed the same way.
Contrast
A comparison between means, written as a sum in which each mean is multiplied by a weight and the weights add up to zero. The simplest contrast is the difference between two means, such as the mean score at 1 year minus the mean score before surgery.
Convergence
Many models have no formula for their estimates, so software starts from a first guess and improves it in repeated steps, called iterations. The fit has converged when a further step makes virtually no difference. If the software reports that a model did not converge, the search stopped before reaching that point.
Correlation
A measure of how closely two variables move together. The correlation coefficient runs from −1 (as one rises the other falls, perfectly) through 0 (no correlation) to 1 (they rise together, perfectly). It says nothing about whether one causes the other.
Cox regression
A regression model for time-to-event data, also called the Cox proportional hazards model. It relates the hazard to one or more predictors at once and gives a hazard ratio for each, without assuming how the hazard itself changes over time. It does assume proportional hazards.
Cramér’s V
An effect size for the association between two categorical variables in a contingency table, from 0 (no association) to 1 (perfect association). It is calculated from the chi-square statistic, the number of observations and the number of rows or columns, whichever is smaller. For a 2 × 2 table it is the same as phi (φ).
Cumulative incidence
The probability that a given event has happened by a given time. With competing risks, the cumulative incidence function (CIF) is the probability of having the event by that time and before any competing event; 1 minus the Kaplan-Meier estimate overstates it. Without competing risks the two are the same.
Degrees of freedom (df)
Roughly, the number of independent pieces of information behind a part of a calculation. For the noise, it is the sample size minus the number of quantities already estimated; for a comparison, it is the number of groups or categories minus 1 (in a table: rows minus 1, times columns minus 1). Software uses it to pick the reference distribution for a test statistic.
Deviance
A measure of how badly a model fitted by maximum likelihood fits the data: the smaller, the better the fit, and a model that fitted perfectly would have 0. The null deviance belongs to a model with no predictors and the residual deviance to the fitted model. The drop from one to the other is the likelihood ratio test statistic.
Distribution
The pattern of how a variable’s values are spread out: where their center lies, how widely they vary and whether the shape is symmetric or skewed. A histogram shows it. Named distributions, such as the normal or the binomial, are mathematical models of such a pattern that give the probability of each range of values.
Dunn’s test
A post-hoc test used after the Kruskal-Wallis test to compare the groups in pairs. It compares the groups’ mean ranks, with the ranks taken from all the values together and not worked out again for each pair. The p-values are then adjusted for multiple comparisons, for example with Holm’s method.
Effect size
A number that says how large a difference or an association is. The p-value doesn’t say that.
Efron’s method (tied times)
A way for Cox regression to handle ties: events recorded at exactly the same time. R’s survival package and Python’s lifelines both use it. It is more accurate than the simpler Breslow method when there are many ties; when there are no ties, the methods give the same result.
Epsilon squared (ε²)
An effect size for the Kruskal-Wallis test: the test statistic H divided by the number of observations minus 1. It runs from 0 to 1, and larger values mean larger differences between the groups.
Equal variances
The assumption that the values are spread out equally in every group being compared, that is, that the groups have the same variance (and so the same standard deviation) in the population. The ordinary (Student’s) t test and ANOVA assume it; Welch’s test does not. In regression the matching assumption is that the residuals have the same spread throughout (constant variance).
Estimate (point estimate)
A number calculated from the sample that stands in for an unknown true value in the population, such as a mean, a difference between two means or a regression coefficient. A point estimate is that single number, as opposed to the confidence interval around it. An estimator is the method that produces it, such as the Kaplan-Meier estimator.
Estimated marginal means
The mean outcome that a fitted model predicts for each group or visit, averaged evenly over the other predictors in the model. They come from the model and not straight from the raw data, so a different model gives different values. Also called EMMs or least-squares means.
Eta squared (η²)
An effect size for ANOVA: the share of the total variation in the outcome that goes with group membership. Partial eta squared sets aside the variation due to other factors, such as the differences between patients in a repeated-measures design; generalized eta squared keeps those differences between patients in, so that different study designs can be compared. Omega squared is eta squared corrected for its tendency to come out too high in a sample.
Event
What a time-to-event analysis waits for: something that happens at one moment, such as revision of an implant, failure of a graft or death. A study has to say exactly what counts as the event and when the clock starts.
Exact (and approximate) method
An exact method works out a p-value or confidence interval from the probability of every result the data could have given, so it is valid at any sample size; Fisher’s exact test and the binomial test are examples. An approximate method (software also says asymptotic or large-sample) uses a normal or chi-square distribution as a shortcut and is accurate only when the sample is large enough.
Expected count
The count a table cell would hold if the null hypothesis were true. In a table of groups by outcome, that means the outcome has nothing to do with the group: row total × column total ÷ grand total. When one group is compared with given proportions, it is the total × the category’s proportion.
F statistic
A test statistic that is the ratio of two variances. In ANOVA it is the variation between the group means divided by the variation within the groups: close to 1 when the groups do not differ, and larger when they do. The F distribution, which has two degrees-of-freedom values (one for each variance), turns it into a p-value.
False discovery rate
Of all the results called significant in a set of tests, the expected share that are false positives. Controlling it, as the Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment does, is a less strict standard than controlling the family-wise error rate.
False negative (type II error)
A result that is not statistically significant when there truly is a difference: chance kept the sample from showing a statistically significant difference. For a true difference of a given size, its probability is called beta, and power is 1 minus beta. Statisticians call it a type II error.
False positive (type I error)
A statistically significant result when there is truly no difference: chance alone made the sample look as if there were one. A test at the 0.05 significance level gives a false positive in 5% of studies in which there is truly no difference. Statisticians call it a type I error.
Family-wise error rate
The chance of at least one false positive across a whole set (family) of tests when there are truly no differences. Adjustments such as Holm’s and Bonferroni’s keep it at or below the chosen level, usually 5%.
Fine-Gray regression
A regression model for competing risks that relates predictors to the cumulative incidence of one event. Its results are subdistribution hazard ratios, which are not ordinary hazard ratios: patients who have had a competing event stay in the at-risk group. It suits the question of how many patients will have the event.
Fisher’s exact test
An exact test of whether two categorical variables in a contingency table, most often a 2 × 2 table, are associated. It keeps the row and column totals fixed and adds up the exact probabilities of the tables at least as extreme as the one observed, so it also works when the counts are small.
Fixed effect
The part of a mixed model that describes the effect of a predictor of interest, such as treatment or visit, on the average patient. A fixed effect is the same for every patient, unlike a random effect.
Follow-up
The time during which a patient is observed in a study, from the starting point (such as the date of surgery) until the event or the last date on which their status is known. A patient who is lost to follow-up stopped being observed before the study ended, and is censored at the last date known.
Friedman test
A rank-based (nonparametric) test for three or more measurements on the same patients, or on matched sets. It ranks each patient’s own values from lowest to highest and asks whether some visits tend to rank higher than others, so differences between patients do not affect it.
Games-Howell test
A post-hoc test that compares every pair of group means without assuming that the groups have similar spreads. It is a version of Tukey’s HSD that uses Welch’s correction, and its p-values are already adjusted for multiple comparisons.
Gray’s test
A test that compares the cumulative incidence of an event between two or more groups when there are competing risks. It does for cumulative incidence curves what the log-rank test does for survival curves.
Greenhouse-Geisser correction
A correction for repeated-measures ANOVA when sphericity cannot be assumed. It reduces the degrees of freedom according to a number called epsilon, which is 1 when sphericity holds and smaller the more it is violated, and the smaller degrees of freedom give a larger p-value. The Huynh-Feldt correction is a less conservative alternative.
Hazard
The moment-to-moment risk of the event among those still at risk: the rate at which events are happening at a given time. It can rise or fall over time. The baseline hazard of a Cox regression is the hazard when every predictor is zero, which the hazard ratios then multiply.
Hazard ratio (HR)
One group’s hazard divided by the other’s. The hazard is the moment-to-moment risk of the event among those still at risk, so a hazard ratio of 2 means the event is twice as likely at any moment.
Hedges’ g
Cohen’s d with a small correction. Cohen’s d tends to come out a little too large in small samples, and Hedges’ g removes that bias; in larger samples the two are almost the same.
Histogram
A plot that shows the distribution of one variable. The range of values is cut into intervals of equal width, called bins, and a bar over each bin shows how many values fall in it.
Hodges-Lehmann estimate
A rank-based estimate that goes with the Wilcoxon and Mann-Whitney tests. For one group (or one set of paired differences) it is the median of the averages of every pair of values, also called the pseudomedian, which equals the median when the distribution is symmetric. For two groups it is the median of the differences between every value in one group and every value in the other, not the difference between the two medians.
Holm adjustment
A way of adjusting p-values for multiple comparisons. The smallest p-value is multiplied by the number of tests, the next smallest by one fewer, and so on, and no adjusted p-value may be smaller than the one before it. It controls the family-wise error rate as the Bonferroni correction does but is never stricter, so it is preferred.
Hypothetical value
The fixed number that a one-sample test compares your group’s mean, median or proportion with, such as a target or a published figure. It comes from outside your data, from theory or from earlier studies, and is not calculated from the data being tested. Also called the comparison value.
Independent observations
Observations are independent when whatever makes one value higher or lower by chance affects that value only. Two hips of the same patient, or several scores from the same knee, are not independent, because what affects one is likely to affect the other. Most tests assume independent observations; when values are related, the analysis has to account for it.
Interaction
Two predictors interact when the effect of one on the outcome depends on the value of the other: for example, when the difference between two treatments is not the same at every visit. A model allows for this with an interaction term.
Intercept
The value a regression model predicts for the outcome when every predictor is 0; for a categorical predictor, 0 means the reference group. It often has no practical meaning of its own, because a predictor such as BMI is never 0.
Interquartile range (IQR)
The range that holds the middle half of the values, from the 25th percentile to the 75th.
Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)
A measure of reliability, usually between 0 and 1: the share of the total variation in the measurements that comes from real differences between patients, the rest being measurement error. Values close to 1 mean the measurement tells patients apart well. In a mixed model the same share describes how alike repeated measurements from one patient are.
Kaplan-Meier method
A way to estimate survival over time when some patients are censored, also called the product-limit method. At each time an event happens, it takes the share of the patients at risk who get past that time without the event, and multiplies these shares together. Each patient counts for as long as they were followed, and the curve steps down only at an event.
Kendall’s W
An effect size for the Friedman test: the Friedman statistic rescaled to run from 0 to 1. It measures how closely the patients agree in the order in which they rank the repeated measurements (for example the visits): 0 means no agreement and 1 means complete agreement.
Kruskal-Wallis test
A rank-based (nonparametric) test that compares three or more unpaired groups. It ranks all the values together, ignoring the groups, and asks whether the groups’ ranks differ more than chance would explain. It extends the Mann-Whitney test to more than two groups.
Least squares
The usual way to fit a regression line or curve: choose the one for which the sum of the squared residuals is as small as possible. For linear regression a formula gives the answer directly (ordinary least squares); for nonlinear regression software has to search for it in repeated steps (nonlinear least squares).
Levene’s test
A test of whether two or more groups have equal variances. A small p-value is evidence that the variances differ.
Likelihood ratio test
A test that compares two models, one with and one without the predictors being tested, by how likely each makes the observed data. A small p-value says the fuller model makes the data more likely than chance alone would explain. In logistic regression its test statistic is the drop in deviance between the two models.
Likert scale
A survey question answered by picking one of a few ordered choices, typically 5 or 7, classically to say how much one agrees or disagrees with a statement. The answers are ordinal: they can be ranked, but the distance from one choice to the next is not necessarily equal.
Linear regression
A model that describes a continuous outcome as a straight-line function of one predictor (simple linear regression) or of several predictors added together (multiple linear regression). It gives a coefficient for each predictor and is fitted by least squares.
Linear relationship
A relationship in which the outcome changes, on average, by the same amount for each one-unit increase in the predictor, so the points on a scatter plot follow a straight line with some scatter around it. Linear regression and the Pearson correlation measure only this kind of relationship: they can show nothing even when two variables are closely related along a curve.
Log-log confidence interval
A confidence interval for a Kaplan-Meier survival probability that is worked out on a transformed scale (the logarithm of minus the logarithm of the survival probability) and then converted back. Because of the transformation, the limits cannot fall below 0% or above 100%, as the plain interval of the estimate plus and minus about 2 standard errors can. In R it is conf.type = “log-log” in survfit(); lifelines uses it for its Kaplan-Meier intervals.
Log-rank test
A test of whether the survival curves of two or more groups differ, also called the Mantel-Cox test. At each time an event happens, it works out how many events each group would be expected to have if the groups did not differ, then compares the totals observed and expected. For two groups, R and lifelines compute it as the Mantel-Haenszel calculation on the 2 × 2 table of each event time, and it is also called the Mantel-Haenszel test for survival data. A statistics guide that tells the two names apart says they differ only in how events at the same time are handled. It gives a p-value but no effect size.
Logarithm (log scale)
The power to which a base must be raised to give a number: the log to base 10 of 100 is 2. In statistics, log usually means the natural log, whose base is e, about 2.718. Equal steps on a log scale mean equal ratios, which pulls in a long right tail, so skewed data and ratios are often analyzed there; zero and negative numbers have no logarithm.
Logistic regression
A regression model for a yes/no outcome, such as revised or not revised. It estimates the probability of the outcome from one or more predictors and gives each predictor’s effect as an odds ratio.
Long and wide format
Two ways to lay out repeated measurements. Wide format has one row per patient, with each repeated measurement in a column of its own; long format has one row per measurement, so a patient with several visits has several rows. Reshaping turns one layout into the other.
LOWESS (LOESS)
A smoothing method that draws a curve through a scatter plot without assuming any formula for its shape. For each point it fits a small regression to the nearby points only, giving the closest ones the most weight, and joins the results into a smooth curve.
Main effect
The effect of one variable in a model on its own, averaged over the levels of the other variables: for example, the difference between two treatment arms averaged over all visits. When there is an interaction, the effect of one variable depends on the level of the other, so a main effect on its own can mislead.
Mann-Whitney test
A rank-based (nonparametric) test that compares two unpaired groups. It asks whether values in one group tend to be larger than in the other: if the groups did not differ, a patient picked at random from one group would have the higher value half the time. Also called the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.
Matched
Chosen to resemble each other: each patient in one group is picked to be like a patient in the other in characteristics such as age and sex. Groups are also called matched when the same patients are measured several times. Matched data are usually analyzed with methods for matched sets, not methods for independent groups.
Maximum likelihood
A method for estimating a model’s numbers: software picks the values under which the observed data were most likely to occur. The likelihood is that probability of the observed data, which changes with the values tried. It is the standard method for logistic and Cox regression.
McNemar’s test
A test for a yes/no outcome measured twice in the same patients, or once in each member of a matched pair. It uses only the discordant pairs, those whose two answers differ, and asks whether changes in one direction are as common as changes in the other.
Mean
The average: add up the values and divide by how many there are.
Mean difference
The mean of one group minus the mean of the other, in the units of the measurement, such as points or degrees. It says how much higher or lower one group is on average. For paired data it is the mean of the differences within the pairs, and in a one-sample test it is the group’s mean minus the hypothetical value.
Measurement error
The difference between a measured value and the true value. Because of it, repeated readings of the same knee differ a little from each other although the knee has not changed. It can be a steady shift (bias) or random scatter; reliability statistics such as the ICC compare it with the real differences between patients.
Median
The middle value when the values are sorted: half lie below it and half above.
Minimal clinically important difference (MCID)
The smallest change in a score, such as a PROM, that patients perceive as beneficial. A difference can be statistically significant and still be smaller than the MCID. Published MCIDs for the same score differ with the patients studied and the method used to work them out.
Missing data
Values that were never recorded, such as an unanswered question or a missed visit. R shows a missing value as NA and pandas as NaN. Whether they bias a result depends on why they are missing; missing at random means that the chance a value is missing depends only on things that were measured, not on the missing value itself.
Mixed model
A model for data in which several measurements belong together, such as scores from the same patient at several visits. It combines fixed effects, which describe the average effect of predictors such as treatment or visit, with random effects, which allow each patient their own level.
Model
A short mathematical description of how an outcome depends on one or more predictors, plus a part for the chance variation that is left over. Fitting a model means having software find the values of its unknown numbers that match the data best; the result is the fitted model.
Multiple comparisons
Running several tests in one study, for example comparing every pair of groups or testing many outcomes. The more tests, the greater the chance that at least one gives a false positive. An adjustment makes each test stricter by raising its p-value (to an adjusted p-value), so that the overall chance stays at the chosen level.
Multivariable model
A model with several predictors at once, also called multiple regression, so that each predictor’s effect is estimated with the others held fixed: adjusted for them. A univariable model has only one predictor.
Nonlinear regression
Regression that fits a curve whose formula you choose, such as a recovery curve that rises quickly and then levels off, where a straight line cannot describe the relationship. The program starts from rough guesses that you supply and improves them step by step until the curve fits the data best (least squares); with poor starting guesses it may fail to find an answer.
Nonparametric (rank-based) test
A test that does not assume the values follow a normal distribution. Most nonparametric tests work on the ranks of the values instead of the values themselves; the Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon signed-rank, Kruskal-Wallis and Friedman tests are examples. Also called distribution-free.
Normal (Gaussian) distribution
A symmetric, bell-shaped distribution: values pile up around the mean and thin out evenly on both sides. It is fully described by its mean and standard deviation, and about 95% of values lie within 2 standard deviations of the mean. Many tests assume that the data follow it.
Null hypothesis
The assumption a test starts from: that there is no difference or no association. The p-value is calculated as if it were true.
Observational study
A study in which the researchers do not assign the treatment; they record which treatment each patient got and what happened afterwards. Patients who got one treatment can differ from those who got another, for example in age or health, so a difference in outcome may not be due to the treatment (confounding).
Odds
The probability that an event happens divided by the probability that it does not: in a group, the number of patients with the event divided by the number without it. Odds of 0.1 mean 1 patient with the event for every 10 without, a risk of about 9%. The log odds, the logarithm of the odds, can take any value and are the scale on which logistic regression works.
Odds ratio
The odds of the outcome in one group divided by the odds in the other. The odds are the number with the outcome divided by the number without it. An odds ratio is not a risk ratio: it lies further from 1, and the two differ most when the outcome is common, so reading an odds ratio as a risk ratio overstates the effect.
Ordinal
Describes data whose values are categories with a natural order, such as mild, moderate and severe, or satisfaction from 1 to 5. The order has meaning, but the steps between neighboring categories are not necessarily equal.
Outcome
The variable a study sets out to explain, predict or change with a treatment, such as a score 1 year after surgery or whether a hip was revised. Also called the dependent variable, the response or the end point.
Outlier
A value that lies far from the rest of the values. Look into it before deciding what to do: it may be a recording error, or it may be real and informative.
Overall (omnibus) test
A single test of whether there is any difference at all among three or more groups, such as the F test of an ANOVA. A small p-value says that some difference exists somewhere, but not which groups differ. Also called an omnibus or global test.
p-hacking (data dredging)
Trying different analyses, outcome definitions or subgroups, or adding more data, until a result becomes statistically significant, and then reporting only that result. The chance of a false positive is then higher than the 5% a single planned test allows. Also called data dredging.
p-value
The probability of a result at least as extreme as yours if there were truly no difference. A small p-value means the data would be surprising if there were no difference. It is not the probability that there is no difference.
Paired
Two measurements that belong together: the same patient before and after surgery, or the left and right hip of one person.
Pairwise comparison
A comparison of two groups at a time. With three or more groups, comparing every pair means several tests (four groups make six pairs), so the p-values need an adjustment for multiple comparisons.
Patient-reported outcome measure (PROM)
A questionnaire that patients answer themselves about their own health, such as symptoms, function or quality of life. The answers come straight from the patient, without a clinician interpreting them, and are often combined into a score.
Pearson correlation (r)
A number, r, from −1 to +1 that says how closely two measurements follow a straight line. Close to +1, one rises as the other rises; close to −1, one falls as the other rises; close to 0, there is no straight-line relationship, though there may still be a curved one.
Percentage point
The unit for the plain difference between two percentages. A fall from 4% to 3% is a fall of 1 percentage point, although relative to the starting 4% it is a fall of 25%.
Percentile
The value below which a given percentage of the values lie. A quarter of the values lie below the 25th percentile.
Planned comparison
A comparison chosen when the study was designed, before any data were seen, because it answers the research question. A few planned comparisons need a milder adjustment for multiple comparisons than comparing every pair, and a single one needs none.
Population
The whole group that a study wants to draw conclusions about, such as all patients who have a hip replacement. It can rarely be measured in full, so a study measures a sample and uses it to estimate the population’s true values, such as the true mean.
Post-hoc test
A follow-up test run after an overall test, such as ANOVA, to find out which groups differ from which. It usually compares the groups in pairs, and because that means several tests, the p-values are adjusted for multiple comparisons. Some authors use the term only for comparisons chosen after looking at the data, as opposed to planned comparisons.
Power
The probability that a study finds a difference statistically significant when a difference of a given size truly exists. It grows with the sample size and with the size of the true difference. An underpowered study is likely to miss a real difference.
Precision
How little an estimate is affected by the play of chance (random error). A narrow confidence interval or a small standard error means a precise estimate; a wide interval means an imprecise one. Precision improves as the sample size grows.
Predictor (covariate)
A variable used to explain or predict the outcome, such as age, sex or implant type. Also called a covariate, an explanatory variable or an independent variable.
Prespecified (and exploratory) analysis
A prespecified analysis is described in the study protocol before the data are seen: which outcome, which comparison, which method. An exploratory analysis is chosen after looking at the data; its results suggest questions for later studies and should be labeled as exploratory. The primary analysis is the prespecified analysis of the study’s main outcome.
Primary outcome
The one outcome that a study names in advance as its most important. It answers the study’s main question, and the sample size calculation is usually based on it. The other outcomes of interest are secondary outcomes; the primary outcome is also called the primary end point.
Probability
A number from 0 to 1, often written as a percentage, that says how likely an event is. A probability of 0.25, or a 25% chance, means the event would happen in about 25 of 100 cases in the long run.
Profile-likelihood confidence interval
A confidence interval for a model coefficient, found by trying one candidate value after another, refitting the rest of the model each time, and keeping the candidates for which the model fits the data not significantly worse than at the best estimate. It need not be symmetric around the estimate, and in small samples it can be more accurate than the Wald interval. R’s confint() gives it for glm() and nls() models.
Propensity score
A patient’s estimated probability of receiving one treatment instead of the other, calculated from characteristics recorded at baseline. In an observational study, patients are matched on this score, or weighted by it (a weighted cohort), so that the treatment groups become alike in those characteristics. It cannot balance characteristics that were not recorded.
Proportion
A part divided by the whole: the number of patients with a characteristic divided by the total number of patients. It lies between 0 and 1 and is often written as a percentage, so 30 of 200 patients is 0.15, or 15%.
Proportional hazards
The assumption behind Cox regression that one group’s hazard is a constant multiple of another’s, so that the hazard ratio stays the same over the whole follow-up. The hazards themselves may change over time. The assumption needs to be checked, for example with Schoenfeld residuals.
Q-Q plot
A plot for checking whether data follow a normal distribution: the sorted values are plotted against the values a normal distribution would give. If the data are roughly normal, the points lie close to a straight line; bends in the line or stray points at its ends show skew or outliers.
Quartile
One of the three values that split the sorted values into four equal parts. The first quartile (Q1) is the 25th percentile, the second is the median, and the third (Q3) is the 75th percentile.
R-squared (R²)
The share of the variation in the outcome that a regression model accounts for, from 0 (none) to 1 (all of it). With one predictor it equals the Pearson correlation squared. Adding predictors can only raise it, so the adjusted R-squared applies a penalty for the number of predictors.
Random effect
The part of a mixed model that lets each patient, knee or other group differ from the average. With a random intercept, all of a patient’s measurements are shifted up or down by that patient’s own amount, and the model estimates how much these amounts vary between patients (a variance component).
Randomized trial
A study in which chance, not the surgeon or the patient, decides which treatment each patient gets. Each treatment group is called an arm. Because of the random assignment the arms are alike on average at the start, even in characteristics nobody measured, so their outcomes can be compared directly to estimate the effect of the treatment.
Rank
A value’s position when all the values are sorted from lowest to highest. Tied values share the average of their positions.
Rank-biserial correlation
An effect size for rank-based tests (Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon signed-rank), from −1 to +1. For two groups, pair every patient in one group with every patient in the other: it is the share of pairs in which the first group’s value is higher minus the share in which it is lower. For paired data, the changes are ranked by size, and it is the share of the rank total that belongs to the increases minus the share that belongs to the decreases.
Reference group
The category of a categorical predictor that all the other categories are compared with, such as the standard implant or the control group. A model reports each other category’s result relative to the reference group, so say which one it is.
Registry
A systematic collection of data on the patients who have a certain kind of operation, such as joint replacement, kept by a hospital, a region or a country. A joint (arthroplasty) registry records the patient, the operation and the implant, and whether the joint is later revised. Registries are used for research, for improving quality and for finding implants that fail more often than others.
Reliability
How well a measurement can tell patients apart despite measurement error: the real differences between patients compared with the differences between repeated readings. It depends on how varied the patients in the sample are, and is usually reported as an ICC. Inter-rater reliability compares different raters, intra-rater reliability the same rater on different occasions, and test-retest reliability the same measurement repeated under the same conditions.
REML (restricted maximum likelihood)
The recommended way to fit a linear mixed model, and the default in R’s lmer and in statsmodels. It is a variant of maximum likelihood that gives less biased estimates of the variances, such as how much patients differ from each other.
Repeated measures
The same outcome measured several times in the same patients, such as a score before surgery and at each follow-up visit. Measurements from one patient are more alike than measurements from different patients, so they are not independent and need a method that allows for this.
Repeated-measures ANOVA
ANOVA for three or more measurements on the same patients, such as a score at several visits, or on matched sets. It first sets aside the differences between patients, so that each patient serves as their own control, and then compares the means of the visits. It assumes sphericity and can use only patients measured at every visit.
Residual
The difference between a patient’s observed value and the value the model predicts for that patient: what is left over after the prediction. A residual plot shows the residuals against the predicted values to check the model’s assumptions. The residual standard error estimates the standard deviation of the residuals: how far, typically, the observed values lie from the predictions.
Retrospective (and prospective) study
A retrospective study uses data that already exist, such as records from routine care: the outcomes have already happened when the study starts. A prospective study is planned first, and the patients are then enrolled and followed forward in time. Both words are used in more than one way, so a paper should say how and when its data were collected.
Reverse Kaplan-Meier method (median follow-up)
A way to calculate the median follow-up, that is, how long patients were followed. It runs the Kaplan-Meier method with the roles swapped: a patient’s observation ending without the event, at the close of the study or through loss to follow-up, counts as the event, and patients who had the event count as censored. When death is not the event studied, a death can count either way, as censored or as the end of follow-up; the two can give different medians, so the Methods section says which was used. The plain median of all follow-up times comes out too short, because patients with an early event stop being followed early.
Risk
The probability that an event, such as a revision or a death, happens within a stated period. It is estimated as the number of patients who had the event in that period divided by the number of patients at its start. That count gives the risk only when every patient was followed for the whole period or until the event: patients who left early are counted as if they never had the event. Otherwise the risk is estimated with the Kaplan-Meier method (1 minus the survival probability) or, with competing risks, as the cumulative incidence.
Risk difference
The risk in one group minus the risk in the other, where each risk is the proportion of patients in that group who have the event. It can be written as a proportion or in percentage points.
Risk ratio (relative risk)
The risk in one group divided by the risk in the other. A risk ratio of 1 means the two risks are equal, and 3 means the event is three times as likely in the first group. It is not the same as an odds ratio; the two differ when the event is common.
Robust
Little affected by outliers or by data that do not quite meet a method’s assumptions. The median is robust to a few extreme values and the mean is not; the t test and ANOVA are fairly robust to mild departures from a normal distribution, especially in large samples.
Robust standard error
A standard error calculated in a way that stays valid when the observations are not independent, for example two joints from the same patient. Ordinary standard errors are then too small. The robust version, also called a sandwich or cluster-robust standard error, corrects this and leaves the estimate itself unchanged.
Sample
The patients (or hips, knees or measurements) that a study actually collected data on. They are one part of a larger population, and what is found in the sample is used to estimate what is true in that population.
Sample size (n)
The number of patients (or hips, knees or measurements) in a study or in one of its groups, written n. The larger the sample size, the more precise the estimates.
Satterthwaite degrees of freedom
A method for approximating the degrees of freedom of the t and F tests on a mixed model’s fixed effects. In a mixed model these test statistics do not exactly follow a t or F distribution, so the method finds the degrees of freedom of one that comes close. R’s lmerTest package uses it to give p-values.
Scatter plot
A plot of two variables against each other, one along each axis, with one point per patient. It shows whether and how the two are related, and whether any point lies far from the rest.
Schoenfeld residuals
Numbers used to check proportional hazards in a Cox regression. For each event and each predictor, the residual is the predictor’s value in the patient who had the event minus the value the model expected. Plotted against time, after Grambsch and Therneau’s rescaling, they should show no trend; a trend means the hazard ratio changes over time.
Scientific notation
How software prints very small or very large numbers. 2.7e-08 means 2.7 with the decimal point moved 8 places to the left: 0.000000027.
Score test
One of three standard tests of a model’s coefficients, with the likelihood ratio test and the Wald test. It is calculated at the no-effect values of the coefficients, so it does not need the fitted estimates. For a Cox model that compares groups, it gives the same number as the log-rank test when no two events happen at the same time; with tied event times the two differ slightly, unless the Cox model handles ties with the exact method.
Sensitivity analysis
A repeat of the main analysis with one choice changed, such as another method, another definition of the outcome or another way of handling missing data. If the result stays about the same, it does not depend on that choice.
Shapiro-Wilk test
A test of whether a sample could have come from a normal distribution. A small p-value is evidence that it did not. A large p-value does not prove that the data are normal: in small samples the test has little power to detect departures.
Sign test
A simple test for one group compared with a hypothetical value, or for two paired measurements, that uses only the direction of each difference: it counts the values above and the values below, and drops those exactly equal. If nothing differed, about half would fall on each side; a binomial test says how surprising the observed split is.
Skewed
Lopsided: most values sit at one end and a long tail stretches out to the other.
Slope
The steepness of a regression line: how much the outcome changes, on average, for each one-unit increase in the predictor. A positive slope means the outcome rises as the predictor rises, and a negative slope means it falls.
Small, medium and large effects (Cohen’s benchmarks)
Labels that call an effect size small, medium (or moderate) or large by comparing it with conventional cut-offs; the best known were proposed by Cohen. They are rules of thumb, not laws: Cohen meant them for use only when nothing better is known, and what counts as a large effect depends on the field and on what matters to patients.
Spearman correlation (ρ)
A rank-based correlation, ρ (rho), from −1 to +1: the Pearson correlation calculated on the ranks of the values instead of the values themselves. It says how consistently one variable rises (or falls) as the other rises, even along a curve, and it suits ordinal data and data that are not normally distributed.
Sphericity
An assumption of repeated-measures ANOVA: the differences between any two visits have the same variance, whichever pair of visits you take. Mauchly’s test checks it, and a small p-value suggests the assumption is violated. If it is violated and nothing is done about it, the p-value of the ANOVA comes out too small.
Spline
A flexible curve built from short curved pieces that join smoothly at points called knots. Putting a predictor into a model as a spline lets its effect bend where a single coefficient would force a straight line. A natural cubic spline, also called a restricted cubic spline, is kept straight beyond the outermost knots.
Spread
How far apart the values in a set of data lie, also called variability. The standard deviation, the interquartile range and the range (the largest value minus the smallest) each put a number on it.
Standard deviation (SD)
The typical distance of a value from the mean. A small SD means the values sit close together.
Standard error
How much an estimate, such as a mean or a difference between two means, would vary from one sample to the next. It shrinks as the sample grows. It is not the SD, which describes how spread out the patients are.
Standardized mean difference (SMD)
The difference between two groups’ means, divided by the standard deviation. It has no units and does not depend on the sample size, so in a Table 1 it shows how far apart two groups are on each baseline characteristic. Cohen’s d and Hedges’ g are versions of it.
Statistically significant
A result is called statistically significant when its p-value is below a threshold fixed before the analysis, the significance level or alpha, which is almost always 0.05 (the 5% level). It means the null hypothesis is rejected because data like these would be unusual if it were true. It does not mean that the difference is large or clinically important. A result that is not statistically significant does not show that there is no difference; the confidence interval shows which differences remain compatible with the data.
Stepwise selection
An automatic way to choose a model’s predictors: software adds or removes one predictor at a time according to its p-value until none is left to add or remove. Its results are unreliable: the p-values of the final model come out too small, its confidence intervals can’t be trusted, and predictors that are only noise often get in.
Stratification
Dividing the patients into groups (strata) that share a characteristic, such as the same surgical approach or the same matched set, and comparing only within each group before combining the results. A stratified Cox regression gives each stratum its own baseline hazard, so it gives no hazard ratio for the variable that defines the strata.
Sum of squares
A measure of variation: take the distance of each value from a mean, square it, and add up the squares. ANOVA splits the total sum of squares into a part between the groups and a part within the groups. A sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom is a mean square, which is a variance.
Survival (survivorship)
The probability of not yet having had the event at a given time after the starting point, such as the date of surgery. When the event is revision, it is the share of implants still unrevised, also called survivorship. A survival curve plots this probability against time. On this site, “survivorship free of revision” and “revision-free survival” are two names for the same estimate: the Kaplan-Meier estimate of this probability with revision as the event, with deaths censored.
Symmetric
Looking the same on both sides of the center: the values lie about equally far below and above the middle. In a symmetric distribution the mean and the median sit in the same place, so the mean is a fair summary. The opposite is skewed.
T-score
A score rescaled so that the average of a reference population is 50 and the standard deviation is 10. PROMIS scores are T-scores, for most measures with the US general population as the reference, so a patient at 40 is 1 standard deviation below that average. It is unrelated to the t test.
t test
A test that compares means: one group’s mean with a hypothetical value (one-sample t test), the means of two separate groups (unpaired t test), or two paired measurements (paired t test). Its test statistic, t, is the difference divided by its standard error: the further t is from 0, the smaller the p-value. Student’s t test is also a name for t tests in general, but on this site it means the unpaired t test that assumes equal variances; the site’s unpaired t tests are Welch’s version, which does not make that assumption.
Table 1
The first table of most clinical papers, which describes the patients who were studied: age, sex and other characteristics, usually as recorded at baseline and shown for each group. It lets readers judge whether the patients resemble their own and how alike the groups were.
Test statistic
A single number, such as t, U or χ², that a test calculates from the data to measure how far they are from “no difference”.
Theil-Sen slope
A slope calculated as the median of the slopes of the lines through every pair of points. It is a robust way to fit a line: a few outliers change it little. Sen’s confidence interval is the confidence interval that goes with it.
Tidy data
Data laid out so that every variable is a column, every observation is a row and every cell holds one value, with each kind of thing observed (patients, visits) in a table of its own. This standard layout is the one statistics software works with most easily.
Ties
Two or more observations with exactly the same value. Rank-based tests give tied values the average of the ranks they share and then correct the test statistic for the ties. In survival analysis, tied times are events recorded at the same time, which Cox regression handles with an approximation such as Efron’s method.
Time-to-event (survival) data
Data that record how long it took until an event happened, such as the years from surgery to revision; the event need not be death. Some patients are censored, so their true time is unknown, and that is why these data need their own methods, together called survival analysis.
Tukey’s HSD
Tukey’s honestly significant difference test: a post-hoc test used after ANOVA that compares every pair of group means. Its p-values and confidence intervals are adjusted for all the pairs at once. It assumes that the groups have similar spreads.
Two-sided (and one-sided)
A two-sided (two-tailed) test counts a difference in either direction as evidence against the null hypothesis. A one-sided (one-tailed) test counts one direction only, which must be chosen before the data are seen; when the difference lies in that direction, its p-value is usually half the two-sided one. A one-sided confidence interval gives only a lower or only an upper limit.
Unit of analysis
The thing that counts as one observation in an analysis, usually one row of the data: a patient, a hip or knee, or a visit. When one patient supplies several rows, such as both knees, those rows are related, and an analysis that counts them as separate patients gives confidence intervals that are too narrow.
Variable
A characteristic that is recorded for every patient and can differ from one patient to the next, such as age, sex or a score. In a data table each variable is one column.
Variance
The standard deviation squared. Tests such as ANOVA compare two variances, and the variances of separate sources of variation, such as differences between patients and measurement error, can be added up to give the total.
Wald confidence interval
The simplest kind of CI: the estimate plus and minus about 2 standard errors (1.96 for a 95% CI). For a ratio, such as an odds ratio or a hazard ratio, the calculation is done on the logarithm of the ratio and the limits are then converted back.
Wald test
A test built from an estimate and its standard error: in its simplest form, the estimate divided by its standard error, compared with a normal distribution. The z and the p-value printed beside each coefficient of a logistic or Cox model are Wald tests. A Wald test can also test several coefficients at once.
Welch’s t test
A version of the unpaired t test that does not assume the two groups have equal variances (the same spread), as the classic version does. Its degrees of freedom are worked out from the data, so they are usually not a whole number. Welch’s ANOVA does the same for three or more groups.
Wilcoxon signed-rank test
A rank-based (nonparametric) test that compares one group with a hypothetical value, or two paired measurements. It ranks the distances from the value (or the paired differences) by size, ignoring their direction, then compares the sum of the ranks above with the sum of the ranks below. It is not the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, which is another name for the Mann-Whitney test.
Wilson interval
A confidence interval for a proportion, also called the Wilson score interval. It collects every candidate value of the true proportion that a test would not rule out given the observed proportion, working out the standard error from each candidate instead of from the sample. Unlike the simple Wald interval, its lower limit cannot fall below 0, and it works well for almost any sample size and proportion.
z statistic (z score)
A z score says how many standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean. A z statistic does the same for an estimate: it is the estimate minus the value expected under the null hypothesis, divided by its standard error, and the normal distribution turns it into a p-value. A test that works this way is called a z test.
